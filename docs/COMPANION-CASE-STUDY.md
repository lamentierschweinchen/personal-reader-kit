# An editorial test: which Westminster Bridge?

This is an original research brief, not an automatically generated tour or a verified live itinerary. It puts a real poem under pressure from edition differences and changing geography. No full text, copied map or photograph is distributed here. Read the poem before opening this whole-poem discussion. Sources were checked on 15 October 2026; current pedestrian access was not established.

## Material and permission

Use William Wordsworth’s Westminster Bridge sonnet in a specifically identified edition. [Project Gutenberg’s volume 1 catalog](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/8774) identifies its text as public domain in the USA. Its reuse notice requires readers elsewhere to check applicable law. The [Academy of American Poets reproduction](https://poets.org/poem/composed-upon-westminster-bridge-sept-3-1802) also labels the poem public domain. This supports a legally reusable research example, not a blanket assertion about every edition or accompanying image. The kit’s `samples/` remains original writing only.

There is already a consequential editorial difference: [Gutenberg’s transcription](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8774/pg8774-images.html), Miscellaneous Sonnets, no. 14, prints a date ending in 1803; the Academy title ends in 1802. Keep the chosen source’s heading and wording intact. Record the disagreement in context rather than silently “fixing” the book. This brief does not establish the manuscript’s exact composition date.

## What the companion should help a reader learn

The intended benefit is to separate a poem’s act of attention from a claim to reconstruct an unchanged view. After using it, the reader should be able to point to one textual choice, one independently established historical change, and one uncertainty. A long essay, three visited stops or a completed checkbox is not evidence that this happened.

Editorial reading: the opening judgment in lines 1–3 addresses how a passer-by attends. Lines 4–8 bring built forms into a morning prospect; lines 9–11 make an experiential comparison; lines 12–14 make movement and stillness coexist. A visit can test what the reader notices in these shifts. It cannot prove the author’s intentions. These line groups refer to the [Academy text](https://poets.org/poem/composed-upon-westminster-bridge-sept-3-1802), not offsets safe to reuse in another prepared edition.

## Evidence that changes the experience

| Layer | Supported claim | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Literary scene | The title names Westminster Bridge; the poem organizes a morning view. | Exact standing point, direction of gaze or each building’s identity. |
| Historical fabric | [Historic England’s official listing](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1066172?section=official-listing) dates the present road bridge to 1862 and attributes it to Thomas Page; it describes cast iron, granite piers and seven arches. | That the present bridge is the fabric encountered in the poem’s early nineteenth-century setting. |
| Contemporary record | [Hansard, 27 February 1862](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1862/feb/27/westminster-bridge-question), records an expectation that the bridge would open early in May, alongside discussion of adjoining houses and approaches. | The actual opening day: a projected date in a parliamentary answer is not proof of completion. |
| Present access | Not verified in this brief. | An open footway, clear pavement, step-free route, suitable stopping point or current permission to enter nearby property. |

The historical difference is useful in its own right. Someone can stand at a recognizably named place while encountering later construction. Ask them to distinguish name, location, material and view. A polished map pin should not collapse those four kinds of continuity into one.

## A bounded field exercise, pending access checks

Propose a short out-and-back along a **currently permitted bridge footway**, beginning and ending on the same bank. These are observation stations along one crossing, not three purported sites from the poem. Before making this an itinerary, verify current pedestrian connections, closures, accessibility, safe stopping space and the reader’s constraints from current official information and on-site conditions. Do not infer them from a heritage listing. If any check fails, keep the seated version below; do not invent a detour.

1. **Before entering the bridge:** reopen lines 1–3. Ask the reader to choose a detail that holds attention without telling them which detail counts as beautiful. Record one observation, with an explicit option to record none.
2. **At a permitted place with a river view:** open lines 4–11 beside the short bridge-fabric note above. Distinguish a present observation from a historical inference. Avoid naming a visible structure as the poem’s specific referent without evidence. Let the reader reject the comparison.
3. **On returning:** reopen lines 12–14. Ask whether movement and stillness can both describe the moment, and whether the historical change sharpened or interrupted the reading. Return to the main book at its unchanged place.

No GPS, external map or route optimization is necessary for this experiment. A seated reader can perform the same evidence comparison with the poem and the cited records. If physical access is later verified, add a dated route receipt and precise passage anchors from the chosen local edition. Cache only rights-reviewed original context; links to source websites still need connectivity.

## Cases where the mechanism should be refused

An occurrence index is a weak default for this short sonnet: the interpretive question above concerns sequence and relationships, not the frequency of a convenient word. Do not pad it with synonyms presented as textual matches. Try adjacent line groups in Facing pages if the reader wants comparison, or leave the poem alone.

A turn-by-turn reconstruction of the poet’s exact viewpoint is also unjustified by these sources. The honest output is a named bridge, a documented later structure and unresolved location details. This is a positive editorial result, not unfinished geocoding.

## Acceptance before promotion

Have a reader first read the poem without the companion, then try the optional exercise. Ask what changed in their attention, which claim they can now support, and which uncertainty remains. Record disagreement and attention cost; remove context that did not help. A subject-competent reviewer should check the interpretation and historical claims. No such reader study or expert review has yet been completed for this brief. It is an explicit test case for the agent’s editorial judgment, not evidence that the kit can automatically produce scholarship or a verified walk.

## Five meetings of writers · 18 October 2026

The owner commissioned five multi-author collections and delegated the reader’s editorial choices to a coordinating agent. Each independent builder prepared faithful reading first, then three working companion studies. The coordinator personally used those studies, followed source visits and selected a narrower production experience. This was agent editorial review, not a human onboarding study or a measured learning-outcome trial. The complete selected witnesses and conversion limits are recorded in each example’s source receipt.

| Collection | Production choice | What the encounter makes available |

| [At Table](../examples/at-table/README.md) | Three ingredient comparisons, with a quieter reading of Estes’s working authorship | The same ingredient leads to a gastronome’s story, a household manual and a professional cook’s recipe. The parallel passages let the reader compare purposes and operations without assigning each author a single social role. The historical works remain literary and social evidence, not updated clinical instructions. |

Several rejected mechanisms were functional. They were removed because their attention cost exceeded what the coordinator found in using them. Recovery remains part of that editorial choice: retiring an experiment must preserve its saved work and a way to export it. Quiet reading does not require completing any study.

Source inspection changed the designs. Beeton’s selected digital text describes engravings without supplying them, so the reader records that omission and links the matched source investigation. It does not borrow pictures from a different expanded edition. Hanslick’s first OCR layer was refused for publication; the historical scan required collation, restored Greek and emphasis, verse notes, physical quotation-continuation handling and stacked figured-bass notation. The original scan leaves remain available. Character agreement, a one-pass visual comparison and complete historical-print proofreading are distinct claims.

Using the actual previews also exposed a reproducible kit defect: a publication test rebuilt the shared preview output with original samples. The kit now isolates that build and proves that an existing preview survives. Other findings belonged to the individual adaptations: source-link attributes colliding with selection attributes, crowded headers at actual 200% interface text, and a Fit image action that fitted width while cropping a tall plate. Those were repaired and checked at the relevant boundaries. An initial direct-link race allegation was withdrawn after the untouched starter recovered the same URL correctly.

For the five covers, an artwork brief identified a shared question, an object and a visual medium. Original vectors supplied a place setting, an aperture, departing line systems, a branch and an empty chair. Each retains its own composition and typography. No new image-generation feature was needed for these particular collections. The existing [design guidance](DESIGN.md) now makes the multi-author artwork decision explicit.

The transferable practice is to inspect the real witness, build materially different studies, use them, and simplify from that evidence. The agent’s own acceptance and automated browser checks do not establish physical-device behavior, screen-reader acceptance, human learning outcomes or comprehensive print proofreading.
