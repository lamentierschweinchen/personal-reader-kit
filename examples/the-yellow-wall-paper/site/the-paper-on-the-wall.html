The Yellow Wall-Paper · A reading

The paper on the wall

Wallpaper, sickrooms, and the nineteenth-century art of resting the eye.

Chapters

  1. The room she wanted
  2. How a pattern holds the eye
  3. A wall for someone who is ill

The narrator of The Yellow Wall-Paper comes to a country house under her physician husband’s care. Forbidden to work, she writes in secret and spends hours in an upstairs room she would not have chosen. There, studying the wallpaper becomes an occupation. She brings to it a little knowledge of design—and a determination to make sense of what she sees.

In the same decades, William Morris and the decorators Rhoda and Agnes Garrett were asking how a pattern might affect the people living with it. Their surviving papers and advice give us another way to consider the narrator’s room: as a place designed for rest, and as a place where a woman has little say over what rest should be.

Discusses later entries in the story.

Chapter one

The room she wanted

Before she describes the yellow paper, the narrator tells us about another room. It opens onto the piazza; roses grow over its window; it has old-fashioned chintz hangings. John refuses it. The room he chooses is upstairs, a former nursery with barred windows, a heavy bed and paper torn from the walls.

She has a preference about her surroundings and says so. Her husband answers with practical reasons: the room downstairs has only one window, too little space for two beds and no nearby room for him.

“I don’t like our room a bit. I wanted one downstairs that opened on the piazza and had roses all over the window, and such pretty old-fashioned chintz hangings! but John would not hear of it.”Charlotte Perkins Gilman, The Yellow Wall-Paper, p. 648

One kind of room is proposed by the person who will live in it; another is chosen for her. The argument about the wallpaper begins before she ever tries to follow its pattern.

Chapter two

Flowers, leaves, and a hidden order

In Marigold, long leaves sweep around large flowers while smaller blossoms fill the dark ground. In Pink and Rose, curved stems carry the eye between the larger flowers; the spaces between them have their own smaller growth. A repeat is there, but the individual leaves are worth looking at too.

Within the pattern

Order without counting

Morris had a practical problem. Wallpaper was printed as a repeating design and laid across a flat wall. If its underlying geometry drew the eye too insistently, he thought, the room would lose its repose. In a lecture delivered in 1881, he advised disguising the construction enough that people would not count the repeats, while still giving the surface clear and beautiful forms. He called the result a “satisfying mystery.”¹

He also warned that accidental lines could appear when a design was repeated across a wall. In his view vertical lines were the worst; diagonal ones were troublesome too. Morris was describing a problem that shows up when the same design covers a wall.

Now return to the narrator. She says the paper repeats by its breadths—the widths in which it is hung—but otherwise seems to obey no principle she knows. Seen one way, the flourishes make separate columns. Seen another, they join diagonally. She tries to find a horizontal order and becomes exhausted.

“But, on the other hand, they connect diagonally, and the sprawling outlines run off in great slanting waves of optic horror, like a lot of wallowing seaweeds in full chase.”The Yellow Wall-Paper, p. 651

The uncertainty is in the movement of her looking. She does not merely announce that the paper is ugly. She keeps testing one account of its structure against another, watching order appear and come apart.

There is a frieze in the nursery too: a horizontal band near the top of the wall. In a fashionable decorating scheme, a frieze, the main wall covering and a lower dado could be different but coordinated papers.² The narrator says a horizontal breadth has been used as a frieze, adding to the confusion.

Chapter three

What should an ill person see?

The London decorators Rhoda and Agnes Garrett advised choosing a bedroom paper with an even, all-over pattern. Someone confined to the room, especially when ill, could be worn out by counting its repeated figures.

“a paper that has an all-overish pattern that cannot be tortured into geometrical figures”Rhoda and Agnes Garrett, Suggestions for House Decoration, London printing, 1877, p. 69

On the next page they recommend chintz or cretonne hangings. They could be washed, cleaned or replaced when they became saturated with London smoke and dust. The narrator’s preferred room already has chintz. Her choice makes sense alongside the Garretts’ idea of a bedroom that is easy to live in.³

Looking becomes work

“Perhaps because of the wallpaper”

The Garretts imagined the labor of counting as a special danger to a sick person. For Morris, a paper might hold the eye without making it labor. Gilman gives that attention a different complication: it becomes exhausting, but it also gives the narrator something to do, to anticipate and to keep to herself.

“I’m getting really fond of the room in spite of the wallpaper. Perhaps because of the wallpaper.”The Yellow Wall-Paper, p. 650

Later she says she is eating better and has something to look forward to. John is pleased by the improvement. She has kept her interest in the wallpaper to herself. The paper can be a source of strain and of absorbing activity at once. A better-looking pattern alone would not settle the question of who gets to decide what helps her.
