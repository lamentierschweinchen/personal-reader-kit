# Two lecture halls, one hour

An optional philosophical double bill, built around choosing a seat and then changing one's mind. Working subtitle: **Schopenhauer and Hegel, across the corridor.**

The doorway is playful; the reading must be serious and welcoming. A spare university noticeboard presents two lecturers scheduled at the same time. Choose a question, take a seat, read an actual passage, then cross to the other hall. The reader can compare, keep passages and leave a private margin note: “What would make me change seats?” The full source editions remain one action away, with independent places and ordinary focus reading.

## Historical premise and editorial boundary

The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's Schopenhauer entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/schopenhauer/) reports that in March 1820 he scheduled his Berlin class at the same time as Hegel's popular lectures and attracted few students. The simultaneous scheduling is the verified seed. Do not invent exact attendance figures, a clock time, adjacent rooms, an exchange of dialogue or a transcript of that day.

Our halls and corridor are an editorial interface, not a reconstruction. The selected texts and English translations were published at different times; label their editions and dates. The comparison must never imply that a later passage was spoken in 1820 or written as an answer to the other selected passage. Schopenhauer's antagonism is not proof of a symmetrical personal feud. A historian's source, a philosopher's accusation and our interpretation are different evidence.

## The first visit

1. **The noticeboard.** A compact invitation, one factual sentence about the timetable clash, and equally prominent entrances for both thinkers. “Read either book” is always available. No autoplay, synthetic lecturer voices, fake student counts or dramatic loading sequence.
2. **Choose a question.** Begin with three candidates: “What is philosophy for?”, “Does disagreement move thought forward?”, and “Does winning an argument bring us closer to truth?” Confirm or replace these after close reading; each must have a substantial, relevant passage on both sides. Do not force a neat opposition where the sources do not supply one.
3. **Take a seat.** Read one faithfully rendered passage with a short, clearly editorial orientation. The book, translator and exact section are visible; fuller context opens without losing the main reading place. Use continuous argument, not a cherry-picked punchline.
4. **Cross the corridor.** Open the paired passage. Tablet can show facing texts; phone should switch compactly between the two, preserving each position and keeping both sources reachable. It must be possible to compare actual words without scrolling past a lecture about how to use the interface.
5. **Leave a margin note.** Keep one or both passages and save a personal connection using edition-aware anchors. An optional prompt asks what challenged the reader's view. No winner, correctness score, public poll or required response.

## A small additional pleasure

An optional **“The quarrel, in the record”** drawer may show a few verified facts and accurately attributed excerpts, with source/date/context. Make it rewarding but secondary. Hegel gets a substantive argument, not merely a role as the target of Schopenhauer's insults. Never invent a retort or present an editorial paraphrase in quotation marks. A dry editorial aside can be funny without pretending either author wrote it.

## Reading material to inspect

- [Hegel, Lectures on the History of Philosophy, volume 1](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/51635), catalogue translator Elizabeth Sanderson Haldane. Its introduction is a promising source for the nature and history of philosophy. Preserve the volume's identity; do not claim it is the complete three-volume work.
- [Schopenhauer, The Art of Controversy and accompanying essays](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10731), translated by T. Bailey Saunders. Distinguish the diagnosis of eristic tactics from an unqualified recommendation to use them.

These catalogues state public domain in the USA. Inspect actual notices, contributors, publication dates, source footnotes and Greek/diacritics before preparing the texts. Record translation rights separately from the philosophers' original works. A different source requires the same review; do not silently switch to a modern translation. Keep the whole chosen volume/collection available to read, and distinguish source prefaces from the authors' text. Start in English with these identified translations; do not manufacture modernized translations.

## Visual and interaction direction

Use the texture of a printed lecture notice through composition and type rather than a literal 3D campus: warm paper, ink, two restrained accents, generous margins, scholarly headings with a little theatrical tension. Both halls share the reliable reading interface but can have their own typographic emphasis. Avoid dashboards, boxing imagery, cartoon portraits, scoreboards and the stock “thesis–antithesis–synthesis” caricature.

Focus hides all the theatre. Retain source title, continuation, intentional selection and controls recalled by deliberate action. Keyboard paths, visible focus, 44px targets, large text and reduced motion remain available. Use SVG icons. No audio or external runtime service is necessary.

## What makes this a useful kit trial

Test cross-work source anchors and saved connections, separate reading positions, exact passage returns, long philosophical sections, source notes and non-Latin text, offline access to both books and companion, and complete backup/restore. Check the educational claim by reading the comparisons: what precise difference can a person explain afterwards, and are both arguments represented fairly? Passing clicks, word counts or an attractive opening screen cannot answer that.
